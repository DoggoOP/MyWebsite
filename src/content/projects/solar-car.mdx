## AutoCFD

I built AutoCFD so that every body shape and wind angle runs the same way. The pipeline takes a vehicle CAD file, sets up a Luminary Cloud simulation, runs and monitors the job, and pulls out drag, side force, lift, center of pressure and convergence history. Results log to a shared Google Sheet so geometry and wind cases line up side by side, and a dashboard tracks running jobs. The setup includes far-field and mesh generation and optional rotating wheels, with the k-ω SST turbulence model and γ-Reθ transition.

AutoCFD is now how the whole aero team runs CFD. I use the pipeline for the aerobody, cooling and drag studies across sealed, leaking and vented shell designs, and for the sail work below.

The same run can also call **Shellpower**, a solar-array layout tool, on the exported body mesh. Shellpower places cells inside a 6.0 m² array budget, ray-casts exposure and shading across sampled headings and hours of the race day, and estimates peak power and daily energy. Running aero and solar together matters for the sail, because a sail changes both the drag and the shadow on the array.

## A sail for crosswinds

A solar car at race speed sees a crosswind at a small apparent angle, which a sail can turn into forward force. I designed a retractable sail that sets camber and trim for the apparent wind and stows when the extra side force, rolling resistance and array shading would cost more energy than the sail saves.

### Sizing from CFD

For one working route case (a 3 m/s southeast wind over the first 40% of the route), the car sees about 26.8 m/s of apparent wind at 4.5°. I ran the base car and four sail shapes through AutoCFD at a 90° crosswind and added the extra rolling resistance from each case's side force:

| Configuration | Drag (N) | Side force (N) | Added rolling resistance (N) | Net resisting force (N) | Change from base (N) |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Base car | 32.10 | 196.00 | 3.35 | 35.45 | 0.00 |
| Fin mast | 14.89 | 299.00 | 7.80 | 22.69 | −12.76 |
| Fat fin | −1.97 | 402.79 | 14.16 | 12.19 | −23.27 |
| AC75 profile | 2.91 | 375.86 | 12.33 | 15.24 | −20.21 |
| AC75 profile + jib | −6.65 | 493.39 | 21.24 | 14.59 | −20.87 |

Negative drag means the sail pulls the car forward. Every sail shape cut the net resisting force by more than a third, and the side force rose with the gain, so the choice came down to how much side load the car could carry. The loads for the design came from these runs: a 260 kg car, a 550 N worst-case side force, the center of pressure from the CFD, and an actuator sized for 16.25 N·m of boom torque under a 130 N side load. The current estimate is 0.88 kWh per day gained against 0.20 kWh per day lost to shading and drag.

### Getting the sail on the car

A sail on a solar car raises fair questions, starting with safety and whether the driver can still get out fast. Convincing the team took a lot of work: I took the design through thorough design reviews covering safety, driver egress and integration with the rest of the car, and after those reviews the team approved the sail for the car.

The next step is the control map: for each apparent wind speed and direction, whether to deploy, what angle of attack to command, and how much camber to set, with net race energy as the objective. Anemometer data from the lead car will supply the real wind distribution.
