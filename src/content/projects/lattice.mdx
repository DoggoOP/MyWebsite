## What Lattice does

Lattice records a scene from several synchronized, calibrated cameras and rebuilds the scene in metric 3D for every frame. One capture gives multiview and egocentric video, human and object motion, pose, and views from angles no camera occupied, all from the same measured geometry. We recorded the capture above with Lattice. Drag to orbit.

I co-founded Lattice with Anton and Yair after TreeHacks. The hackathon build streamed live 3D to a HoloLens. The company now builds capture rigs that record people doing physical work, so robotics teams can train on real motion with correct 3D geometry.

## The TreeHacks build

Three Kinect v2 sensors each fed a client laptop, because the Kinect SDK allows one sensor per PC. The C++ clients streamed color and depth over TCP to a C# server that calibrated the sensors, fused the three clouds, buffered the timeline, and served the result to three viewers on separate ports: a HoloLens 2 app with pinch, grab and voice control, a browser viewer with touch controls, and a desktop viewer with Leap Motion hand control. We started from LiveScan3D, an open-source Kinect capture system [3], and built the replay, annotation and multi-viewer layers on top.

### Calibration

We first calibrated with PnP: detect the printed marker in each camera's color image and solve for that camera's pose. During the hackathon those poses were not accurate enough to make the three clouds line up, so we switched to aligning in 3D. Each Kinect measures the marker in the camera's own depth data, an Orthogonal Procrustes solve [1] gives the rigid transform from that camera to the marker frame, and ICP [2] refines the overlap afterward. Procrustes returned flipped solutions when two cameras faced the marker from 180° apart, so we added checks that reject bad rotation candidates and test each solution against the other cameras.

### Keeping a 3-sensor stream live

- **Backpressure.** Ingest, fusion, compression and playback each ran at a different rate. Bounded queues and ring buffers that drop stale frames stopped one slow stage from stalling the rest.
- **Memory.** The stack crosses C++ and C#, and garbage-collection pauses showed up as playback hitches. We cut allocation churn and reused pinned buffers across frames.
- **Point throughput.** Reworking the point layouts for SIMD, plus frustum culling and level of detail, kept fusion and rendering inside the frame budget.
- **Sensor interference.** The Kinects' infrared projectors interfered with each other during calibration, so sensor placement became part of the calibration procedure.

## Lattice now

The current rig uses four ZED X stereo cameras on GMSL2, with hardware sync under 100 µs. A Jetson AGX Orin only captures and encodes, then sends frames over a 25 GbE link to a workstation with two RTX 5090s, which runs depth, reconstruction and streaming. Viewers connect through a Cloudflare MoQ relay.

**Reconstruction.** A 26-stage CUDA pipeline fuses the cameras, meshes the surface, completes missing depth and completes occluded body parts. Each camera runs in a separate worker process under MPS, and the pipeline pairs frames by hardware timestamp, because sequence counters drift once processes run independently. A TSDF voxel only meshes after three integration passes agree, which removes the ghost surfaces that moving people leave behind. Depth fill runs at 3.5 ms per frame.

**Calibration.** We calibrate the rig once with a ChArUco board, and a self-calibration step runs on every take after that. I designed the self-calibration residual around pixel-ray agreement between cameras instead of depth-to-depth ICP. The pipeline computes depth from the calibration, so using depth to estimate the calibration converges to a self-consistent wrong answer. ICP now only audits the result.

**Body tracking.** The pipeline triangulates 2D keypoints from each camera with a weighted least-squares ray intersection, then fits a body model with about 130 degrees of freedom using Levenberg-Marquardt. LM brought the fit error from 111 mm to 0.17 mm in six iterations, while Adam diverged on the same problem. A Gauss-Newton correction runs every render frame in under 1.5 ms for two people.

**Streaming.** An exact encoding of the reconstruction needs 5.7 Gbps on a link of about 700 Mbps. The custom splat format packs each splat into 0.905 bytes, about eight times smaller, with a median normal error of 5.7° or less, using quantization along each camera ray and context-modeled rANS entropy coding. Splat order turned out to be a compression parameter: one reordering cut neighbor availability from 97.5% to 18.1% and turned a 6.9% gain into a 6.9% loss.

### Does 3D capture make better robot data?

I built our benchmarking, which asks whether multi-camera 4D capture is better robot training data than the best single camera from the same rig. I pre-register each link in the chain, and a held-out referee camera that neither source used grades each link, with a paired z above 2 to pass.

- **Measurement.** Cloth reconstruction is 37.8% more accurate (30.3 mm against 60.0 mm). About a third of the gain comes from the extra cameras' cloth points and the rest from the multi-camera body and hand fit.
- **Usable data.** Single-camera hands leave gaps inside contacts, so they cannot drive a cloth twin. When we retarget the demos to a Unitree G1, 60 of 100 Lattice demos replay, against 25 of 100.
- **Policy.** A Diffusion Policy that we trained on Lattice demos succeeds on 109 of 150 episodes against 82 of 150 (z = 3.36). In an Isaac Lab-Arena pick-and-place task, Lattice data scores 0.73 against 0.55 for the best single camera, still wins when we match the demo count, and pooling all four single cameras without fusion only reaches 0.43, so the gain comes from the cross-camera fusion. Shifting the actions one second late drops the score to 0 of 50, which shows the benchmark can tell good data from corrupted data.
- **World model.** Fine-tuning NVIDIA's PointWorld on Lattice data lands 6.4% closer to the referee camera than the public model and 47.3% closer than single-camera data.

The negative results stay on the record. On a cloth-fold simulation benchmark, our fused reconstruction lost to a single raw camera (44.1 mm against 26.9 mm surface divergence). I ruled out three confounds with evidence before writing the cause down as unknown; the leading idea is that more accurate surface relief is not something the simulator handles well. A cloth world model that we fine-tuned on our data failed four registered tests, and I traced the failures to the model's training targets, which drift 7 to 26 mm above the measured cloth.

### NVIDIA V2D challenge

Track 1 of NVIDIA's V2D challenge asks for a full human-object interaction (body, object shape and pose, metric scale, one world frame) from a single uncalibrated RGB video. We built our own method instead of adopting NVIDIA's baseline: one persistent world state for the person, object and room, which we fit jointly over each episode. We weight every residual (body measurement, object track, silhouette, contact, no interpenetration, smooth motion) by that source's measured noise instead of hand-tuned rules.

$$\min_{x}\sum_k \frac{1}{\sigma_k^2}\|r_k(x)\|^2 \quad \text{subject to no penetration}$$

Our submission ranked 2nd of 56 teams on human joint acceleration error and 5th on object acceleration error, and beat NVIDIA's CARI4D baseline on all five boards: 93% lower human acceleration error, 91% lower object acceleration error, and 17% and 24% lower Chamfer distance for the human and object meshes.
