## Overview

Every upwind leg in a race is a trade. Pointing closer to the wind covers less distance but the boat is slower; footing off is faster but sails farther. On top of that, every tack costs speed, and the wind shifts and gusts across the course. I raced through those calls for years, so I wanted to see whether an agent could learn them from physics alone. I simulated the sail aerodynamics from first principles, then trained a reinforcement learning agent to find the fastest route to an upwind mark through a shifting wind field.

The pipeline has three stages:

1. A Lattice Boltzmann wind tunnel in Wolfram Mathematica computes lift coefficients for a two-sail rig (mainsail + jib) across combinations of wind speed (5–20 knots), boat angle (37.5°–52.5° off the wind), and sail angle (0°–15°)
2. A PPO agent navigates a 1000×1000 grid with a spatially varying wind field, using the precomputed lift data to determine boat speed at each heading
3. The trained agent's path is animated over the wind vector field in real time

Sail shapes are defined using Bézier curves with manually tuned control points, constrained to match real-world arc lengths:

| Sail | Arc Length | Chord |
|------|-----------|-------|
| Jib | ~169 cm | 163 cm |
| Mainsail | ~199.5 cm | 194 cm |

The Lattice Boltzmann wind tunnel runs both sails simultaneously. At each time step, 8 particles per grid point advect in their respective directions; collisions trigger velocity redistribution. Vorticity fields reveal the pressure differential across each sail, and lift coefficients are extracted at the center of effort of each sail. Total lift is computed as:

$$L = C_{l,\text{main}} \cdot A_{\text{main}} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2 + C_{l,\text{jib}} \cdot A_{\text{jib}} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2$$

where $\rho = 712$ (air density used in simulation), $A_{\text{main}} = 8.673\;\text{m}^2$, $A_{\text{jib}} = 3.738\;\text{m}^2$.

Simulations were run for every combination of wind speed, boat angle, and sail angle, parallelized across kernels. The sail angle giving maximum lift was retained for each (wind speed, boat angle) pair, and in almost every case the best sail angle was 0°.

## Reinforcement Learning Environment

A custom OpenAI Gym environment built on the 1000×1000 coordinate grid. The agent starts and ends at random positions, with a discrete action space of 7 boat angles (37.5°–52.5° off wind) plus a tack action. Wind varies spatially: base speed ranges from 7–18 knots with ±3 knot variation per cell, and direction shifts ±20° per cell.

The observation space is 252-dimensional, encoding:
- Current position, wind speed and direction, angle to goal, distance to goal
- Layline status, tack count, boundary proximity
- Simulated future positions and rewards for all 8 actions
- Full flattened wind field

### Reward Shaping

- Positive reward proportional to VMG (velocity made good toward the goal)
- Time penalty proportional to $100{,}000 / L$ per step, where $L$ is the lift force
- +1,000 for tacking on the layline; −1,000 for tacking off the layline
- −10,000 and episode termination for two consecutive tacks
- +50,000 (minus time penalty) for reaching the goal
- −50,000 for going out of bounds

## Training

I trained the agent with PPO (learning rate 0.003, discount factor 0.95, and an entropy coefficient of 0.1, well above the default, to push exploration) in 100,000-step blocks until the mean reward over 10 evaluation episodes passed 1,000,000.

The reward above is the result of many rounds of tuning by hand. Early agents found three ways to fail. Some never tacked and sailed off on one board until they left the course. Some tacked endlessly, because every tack looked like progress. Some kept sailing without heading for the mark at all. Each failure meant reweighting the terms: how hard to punish a tack, how much speed a tack costs while the boat turns through the wind, and how strongly to reward sailing into stronger pressure so the agent would learn what a gust is worth.

## Results

The trained agent sails upwind in the zigzag a racer would sail: out on the lifted tack, a tack at the layline, and up to the mark. The agent also learned to head for gusts and lifts to cut the distance sailed. The most satisfying result was the layline: the agent learned to sail out to the layline before tacking, the same call a competitive sailor makes.

## Next steps

The sails are rigid Bézier shapes fitted to measured arc lengths, the wind field is synthetic, and the model covers upwind legs only. Next I would add a vang parameter for mainsail twist, a downwind leg with a spinnaker, real race-day wind for post-race route analysis, higher-resolution LBM runs in the cloud, and a full course with multiple marks.
