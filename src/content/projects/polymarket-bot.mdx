## Overview

The bot trades Polymarket prediction markets without supervision, focusing on time-bounded markets with a measurable underlying signal. The bot runs end-to-end on a Railway deployment: data collection, forecasting, edge calculation, order execution, and a live Next.js dashboard. The public dashboard shows performance and bot health; positions and recommendations are private.

Most of the work went into catching the bot doing something other than what I thought, usually while looking profitable in a backtest, and then writing a constraint so the same mistake could not happen again. The architecture below is what survived.

The system is built around four loosely coupled stages, each independently testable and replaceable:

1. **Data collection.** Multi-source polling with fallback chains.
2. **Forecasting.** An ensemble model that maps observed history to a probability distribution over market outcomes.
3. **Edge calculation.** Model probabilities compared against live CLOB prices, with spread modeled.
4. **Execution.** Kelly-sized orders through the Polymarket CLOB API, with a risk cap on every bracket.

## Data Collection

The ingestion layer pulls from multiple sources with fallback chains to handle API outages and rate limits. Raw observations are normalized against a time-of-day-aware baseline so the model always operates on comparable, regime-adjusted quantities across different markets and time windows.

A scheduler polls every 30 seconds and persists everything to SQLite in WAL mode so the dashboard can read concurrently without blocking the trading loop. New markets are auto-discovered hourly through the Polymarket API, with each market's measurement window parsed from the market title. The trading window and the resolution window are often different intervals, so the parser matters.

## Forecasting

The forecast layer is an ensemble: multiple independent signals each produce a projection from observed history, and a learned set of weights blends them into a single distribution over final outcomes. Sigma is fit empirically rather than assumed, and the resulting distribution is mapped onto market brackets via a truncated Normal CDF.

Markets with different resolution horizons behave differently, so the ensemble runs a separate parameter set for each market type, found by the optimizer. A pre-market warm-start uses the last 48 hours of underlying activity to initialize the ensemble before a market opens for trading, so the first prediction the bot makes is already informed.

## Edge & Execution

The trading engine compares the model's bracket probabilities against live order-book prices:

$$\text{edge} = p_{\text{model}} - p_{\text{market}} - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{spread}$$

Brackets with positive edge are bought, sized by a fractional Kelly rule with a per-bracket hard cap so no single position can dominate the portfolio. The engine exits when the model's forecast flips, with trend-aware filters to avoid cutting winners on temporary price moves and a phase-dependent threshold that lets borderline positions ride to resolution rather than churning on noise near bracket boundaries.

Orders route through the Polymarket CLOB API. A paper-trading ledger mirrors the live execution path, so strategy changes run end-to-end before real money goes in. Paper and live mode differ only in which broker the engine talks to.

## Things I Got Wrong

Almost every constraint in this codebase exists because the bot did something stupid and lost money first.

### The backtester wanted to market-make

The first version of the optimizer kept finding strategies that looked phenomenal in-sample. When I dug into the trade logs, the "strategy" the optimizer had converged on was effectively market-making: bidding both sides of every bracket and collecting the spread, with the directional model along for the ride.

I wasn't trying to build a market maker, and the results were extremely sensitive to the simulator's spread assumptions: small changes to the spread model flipped them from profitable to ruinous. I added constraints to prevent the optimizer from exploring that region: minimum edge thresholds with hard floors, no offsetting positions on the same market, and a fitness penalty for trip count above a per-market ceiling. Once those were in, the optimizer had to actually find directional edge or score badly.

### I thought I had a speed advantage

For a stretch I was convinced the bot had an execution-speed edge, and that polling faster and filling ahead of the market consensus was real alpha. I cut the loop interval, tightened the order cycle, and ran the faster loop live. The bot lost money consistently.

The order book on Polymarket is thin and fills are slow. My "speed advantage" was me paying the spread to enter positions slightly before the rest of the market priced them correctly for free. The edge comes from being right about the outcome. I rolled back the polling cadence and the order cycle, and the P&L recovered.

### The bot was gambling

The worst stretch was watching the dashboard show the bot opening positions on brackets where, by my own model, the edge was effectively zero. The minimum-edge threshold had been a tunable parameter, and at some point the optimizer had quietly driven the threshold to zero on the in-sample data, because in the simulator, with perfectly modeled spreads and no real adverse selection, a tiny edge multiplied by Kelly sizing was still net-positive on average.

Live, that is gambling. Model noise puts the bot randomly long on every bracket of every market, churning capital and bleeding money through spread and slippage. I locked the floor: minimum edge can no longer optimize below a hard threshold, the abandonment criterion got tightened so borderline positions get cut early instead of riding noise, and the per-bracket cap got more aggressive so a stupid position that slips through can't grow into a real loss.

All three mistakes share one pattern. The optimizer relentlessly minimizes fitness, and any loophole in the fitness function gets found by the optimizer before I find the loophole myself. Most constraints in this system exist to stop the bot from making money in ways I don't trust.

## Backtester & Optimizer

Historical resolution data feeds a tick-level simulator with realistic spread, price impact, and settlement. A Bayesian optimizer tunes the strategy parameters against a fitness function that multiplies P&L, profit factor, Sortino ratio, drawdown, win rate and per-trade efficiency, so no strategy can trade one weakness for a gain elsewhere.

To prevent overfitting on lucky fold compositions, the optimizer:

- Uses fresh capital for every market, so wipeouts and lucky streaks cannot dominate the score through compounding
- Re-randomizes folds on every evaluation and averages across multiple samples per fold
- Scores `0.6 × worst fold + 0.4 × mean fold`, so a strategy has to hold up on every subset
- Re-evaluates every new "best" on a fresh fold seed and overwrites unstable peaks in-place inside the GP's training set, so the surrogate model learns to distrust outliers instead of chasing them
- Holds out a set of markets the optimizer never sees; the in-sample versus out-of-sample gap on that holdout is the final test

Each of these came out of a specific case where an earlier version of the optimizer found a strategy that looked great on aggregate but was secretly dependent on a single lucky market, a single lucky fold composition, or a compounding artifact. The fitness function and fold logic now exist mostly to distrust results.

## Infrastructure

- **Flask** backend serves predictions, recommendations, portfolio state, and trade history to the dashboard and any external monitoring
- **APScheduler** drives the data + trading loop on a 30-second cadence aligned to market resolution times
- **Next.js** dashboard renders live positions, ensemble projections, market consensus comparison, per-bracket edge, and signal breakdowns
- **Railway** handles continuous deployment from GitHub, environment variable injection, and a persistent volume for the SQLite database
- **Paper and live mode** switch on a single environment variable, with the same execution path and a different broker
