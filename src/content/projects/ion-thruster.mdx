## The question

Electric thrusters reach exhaust speeds that chemical rockets cannot, but they need a large power supply for a small amount of thrust. I wanted to know which geometric parameters of a thruster set that cost, so I built simulations of a gridded ion thruster and a Hall effect thruster and swept their geometry to minimize the power required per newton of thrust.

I did this work at the Wolfram Summer Research Program, with Shenghui Yang as my mentor, and published the results as the sole author in the Journal of High School Science, volume 8, issue 3 (2024).

## Building the simulation

### Geometry and fields

I defined each thruster as implicit regions in Mathematica (anode, screen and accelerator grids, cathode, magnets) and assigned a charge density to each electrode. The gridded thruster starts at 10⁶ C/m³ on the anode, 10⁴ on the screen grid and −10⁵ on the accelerator grid.

<div style="display:grid; grid-template-columns:1fr 1fr; gap:12px; margin:16px 0;">
  <img src="/assets/GridRegions.png" alt="Implicit regions defining the gridded ion thruster electrodes" style="width:100%;" />
  <img src="/assets/HallRegions.png" alt="Implicit regions defining the Hall thruster anode, cathode and magnets" style="width:100%;" />
</div>

The electrostatic potential comes from solving Poisson's equation over that domain with NDSolve:

$$\nabla^2 \phi = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon}, \qquad \mathbf{E} = -\nabla \phi$$

The Hall thruster also needs the magnetic field that traps electrons in the channel, which I computed from the magnet geometry with the Biot–Savart law at 0.02 T:

$$\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \int_C \frac{I \, d\mathbf{l} \times \mathbf{r}'}{|\mathbf{r}'|^3}$$

### Ion trajectories

With both fields in place, I integrated the equations of motion for xenon ions to get each trajectory, the velocity along the trajectory, and the energy each ion gains through the thruster.

### Thrust and power

Exhaust velocity comes from differentiating the interpolated trajectory. Thrust and specific impulse follow from the ion mass flow rate, which depends on ion density, channel cross-section, ion mass and ionization rate:

$$T = \dot{m} \, v_e \, \eta, \qquad I_{sp} = \frac{T}{\dot{m}\, g_0}$$

Power is the sum of ionization power, the kinetic power the thruster gives the ions, and a fixed 150 W electron gun:

$$P = P_{ion} + \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{m} v_e^2 + P_{e^-}$$

## Sweeping the geometry

I wrapped the whole simulation in one function and swept anode radius, thickness and height, grid spacing and grid height for the gridded thruster, and anode radius, thickness and length, cathode radius and inner magnet radius for the Hall thruster. Each parameter ran over a deployable range and a wider idealized range, and correlation maps showed which parameters drive thrust and which drive power.

Thrust scaled strongly with the size parameters, power rose nonlinearly, and several geometric increases made efficiency worse. The optimum came from shrinking the right dimensions:

| Thruster | Geometry change | Exhaust velocity | Thrust | Isp | Power per thrust |
|:--|:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Gridded ion | anode radius 0.40 → 0.25 m, thickness 0.04 → 0.01 m, grid spacing 0.035 → 0.005 m | 9,545 m/s | 0.026 N | 681 s | 14.04 kW/N |
| Hall effect | anode length 0.085 → 0.05 m, cathode radius 0.04 → 0.01 m, magnet radius 0.08 → 0.06 m | 18,613 m/s | 0.186 N | 1,898 s | 10.59 kW/N |

For reference, NASA's NEXT gridded thruster runs at about 30 kW/N and ESA's PPS-1350-G Hall thruster on SMART-1 at about 17 kW/N.

## Checking against data

I compared the Hall thruster's simulated ion velocity profile with University of Michigan PEPL data, which I accessed through the HallThruster.jl package. The simulated exhaust velocity of about 18 km/s landed within 10% of the reference 19.9 km/s.

## Next steps

The model treats ions as already ionized and leaves out plasma friction, heating and erosion. The next version would add the ionization process and a Boris particle pusher, and move to full 3D geometry.
